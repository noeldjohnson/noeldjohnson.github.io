1Argument

Early modern rulers faced two costs. Collecting taxes was expensive in economies that were fragmented and hard to monitor, so rulers sold the right to collect a tax to a private farmer, who paid a fixed lease and kept whatever he collected. Borrowing was expensive too, because a king could not credibly promise to repay, so rulers depended on loans from the people closest to their revenues, including their tax farmers.

The paper builds a model that connects the two. When the crown does not need to borrow, competitive auctions are the best way to allocate tax farms, because bidding reveals which farmer can collect at lowest cost. When the crown needs large loans, competition fails, because a crown can default on any single farmer and re-lease the farm to another. A small group of financiers holding all the farms, a cabal, can punish a default together, so it can lend far more. Both England and France moved from competitive farming to cabal farming as their borrowing grew after 1600.

Cabal farming gave the crown a reason to invest in standardizing laws, taxes, and weights and measures, since it no longer learned collection costs from auctions. That investment undermined the cabal, because a more uniform fiscal system made direct collection a better outside option and lowered the cost of defaulting on the farmers. England, which was more uniform to begin with, abandoned its Customs farm in 1671 and its Excise farm in 1683. France kept its General Farms until the Revolution.

2The model

Tax farmers differ in their cost of collection, which is spread uniformly around a mean. The spread is larger where the fiscal system is more fragmented, and the crown can shrink it by investing in standardization. Under competition the crown gets close to the value of the lowest-cost farmer. Under a cabal the crown and the cabal split the surplus by Nash bargaining, and the crown's threat point is direct collection at the highest cost.

The crown borrows an amount x against future revenue, which the paper calls anticipations. The crown keeps repaying the cabal as long as its discount factor δ is at least (x − ΔV)/(x + ΔV), where ΔV is a quarter of the gap between the farm's value under the cabal and under direct collection. Rearranged, the cabal can lend at most ΔV(1 + δ)/(1 − δ). Under perfect competition the crown defaults on any loan at all.

Two results follow. The crown gets less from a cabal than from competition, so it switches only when it needs to borrow. And the crown gains more from standardization under a cabal than under competition, because an auction already reveals collection costs. Standardization then lowers the spread of costs, which shrinks ΔV and with it the lending a cabal can sustain. The institution that encouraged the investment is undermined by it.

3Two paths through the model

The paper's Figure 3 places England and France on two axes, standardization and inside finance, between 1500 and 1800. Both began with competitive farming and low borrowing. Both moved up into cabal farming as borrowing grew. England started more standardized, so its cabal became unstable sooner and it moved to direct management after 1670. France stayed in the cabal region until the Revolution.

4Lending through the French farms

No French data on lease prices or loans through the farms survive for the sixteenth century. For 1600 to 1656 the records kept by Jean-Roland Malet, published by Richard and Margaret Bonney, give the lease price of each farm and incomplete, and therefore lower-bound, figures on the loans made through them.

Real lease prices rose through 1643 and fell after. Loans through the farms ran from about half the lease price to more than its full value. At least before 1640 the high ratios came from more lending and not from falling leases. Between 1598 and 1655 only a third of leases ran their full contracted length, which is what the model predicts when a crown borrows from competitively allocated farms. The farms were consolidated in two waves, the salt, customs, and sales tax farms between 1578 and 1604, and then under Colbert into the single Company of General Farms by 1681.

5The Great Farm of the Customs

Elizabeth I put the Customs out to competitive farm from the late 1560s. James I was in one historian's words a super-borrower whose demands the London money market could not meet. In 1604 the Great Customs were consolidated into a single farm held by a syndicate that became the leading lender to James I and Charles I.

The rent of the first farm was £112,400. The crown did not push the rent up to what the farm could bear, and Ashton reads the low rent as the price the crown chose to pay for the new services the farmers performed. Under Charles I the crown began anticipating the rent several years ahead, and anticipations rose to almost half the annual rent by the mid-1630s. In 1641 Parliament expropriated the farmers. They were restored in 1660, but in 1671 the Customs farm was cancelled and the Customs placed under government supervision. The Excise followed in 1683. Both changes came before the Glorious Revolution.

6Standardization in France

To measure investment in standardization the paper counts the royal arrêts and edicts that limited five seigneurial institutions. These are the terrier, or seigneurial land register, seigneurial justice, the péages, or local tolls, corvée labor, and weights and measures. The list comes from Renauldon's 1765 treatise on seigneurial rights, and each rule change in an edict counts once.

8Limits

The French loan figures are incomplete, so the ratio of loans to lease price is a lower bound, and no French series exists before 1600.

The paper's Figure 3 is a schematic. The positions of England and France on it are qualitative and the calculator in Section 2 uses one illustrative form of the model.

The arrêt counts come from one treatise written by a seigneur. Renauldon wrote as an aid to other seigneurs facing royal encroachment, so the paper argues his list should if anything be biased against rulings that knocked down traditional rights.

The model explains why cabal farming was less stable in England. The paper also names the contingencies of the 1660s and 1670s, including a quarrel between the Lord Treasurer and the head of the farming syndicate, the costs of the Dutch War, and the king's spending.
