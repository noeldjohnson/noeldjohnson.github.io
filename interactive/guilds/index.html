1Argument

In the year 1000 Ghent held about five thousand people. By 1300 it held sixty-four thousand, and only Paris and London were larger north of the Alps. Its craft guilds enter the surviving record in the thirteenth century, near the peak of that rise. Over the next three centuries the city lost half its people. Read one way, guilds arrived and growth stopped. Read another, guilds were first written down in a city that had been rising for three hundred years.

Much of what economists know about institutions and growth comes from designs that date an institution by its first appearance in the record. Guilds, communes, universities, printing presses, and market rights have all been used this way. The record is built from documents that survived, and cities that grew wrote more and kept more. The paper asks how selected that record is and offers a portable way to measure how severe that selection is in any design that dates a treatment by its first record.

It uses a panel of 1,754 European cities from 1000 to 1800, with documented guild activity in 724 of them. Cities whose guilds are first documented had been about twice the size of their neighbors for four centuries before the record. They also had twice the notable births of cities their own size. Seven other institutions go through the same comparison. The first printing press shows the same long lead. Towns that received a market right or a charter were ordinary towns. Capitals and sees went to large cities.

Once the selection is measured, the guild evidence reads differently. Cross-city regressions imply that a century of guild presence goes with a city twice as large in the long run. Within-city and matched designs put the premium near 14 percent. The apparent decline of guild cities after their guilds appear is mean reversion, which never-documented cities of the same size share.

2The model

An institution comes into being in a town when whoever decides finds it worth the cost. Two dials set how far the towns that acquire it stand above the average town. The first is a threshold, the size a town must reach for the institution to pay. Craftsmen gain from organizing only where there are many of them, so a guild has a high threshold. A ruler selling a market right collects a fee that covers his cost in any town, so a grant has none. The second dial is the number of places available. A kingdom has one capital and a diocese one see.

Move the dials. Town size is lognormal, so the log of town size is normally distributed. The lead is the mean size of the towns that acquire the institution over the mean size of all towns, Eq. 3 in the paper. The figure opens on the size scale, which shows the long right tail that carries the lead. The log scale shows the same distribution as a normal bell.

Scale persists from century to century, which carries the lead backward from the date the institution was decided to the centuries before the record. A guild existed before anyone wrote it down, so its record lags its formation. A charter or a seat is recorded when it is created. 3Where guilds were recorded

Guild documentation begins in the Mediterranean commercial core and northern France. It spreads through the Holy Roman Empire and the Low Countries in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries and reaches the periphery late. Documented and never-documented cities are interleaved everywhere, which is what lets the paper match each guild city to similar cities nearby.

Figure 3. All 1,754 cities in the analysis sample. Filled circles have documented guild activity by the year on the slider and are shaded by the century it was first recorded. Open gray circles do not. The record dates when guilds were active, not when they were founded.

4Which cities enter the record

A lead before the first record could be true of any institution dated this way. To see whether guilds are unusual, the paper puts seven other institutions through the same comparison. Two grew from below like guilds, the first printing press and the commune. Four were conferred from above. Market rights and town charters were granted by a lord, and capitals and new bishoprics and archbishoprics were seats placed in a city.

Each institution is dated by a different source, so each can only be compared within the cities its source covers. The printing press covers the full panel. Market rights and charters come from the Deutsches Städtebuch and cover German and Austrian towns. Communes, capitals, and sees come from Bosker, Buringh, and van Zanden (2013) and cover the cities in their data. Each comparison in Figure 5 therefore shows guilds in the same set of cities as a benchmark.

Two measures are compared. Population is the first. The second is notable births, the number of people born in a city in a century who appear in the database of Laouenan et al. (2022), about two million notable people assembled from Wikipedia and Wikidata biographies. It measures a city's prominence and does not depend on its population estimate. For each institution and each century from four before its first record to four after, the paper compares the cities that acquired it with the cities that did not. The population comparison uses every city in the comparison pool and counts a city with no estimate as small. The notable-births comparison uses cities whose population one century before the event was within a factor of about 2.7 of the event cities' mean, so it measures prominence given size. Per-inhabitant births remove the size difference that remains inside that band.

The guild lead sits in the mean, not the median. The median guild city was the size of the median comparison city in the early window, and the larger guild cities carry the lead. Guild cities were also more often recorded at all, 83 percent against 71 percent in the early window. Counting only recorded cities, the early guild lead is 1.7 rather than 2.0.

5Exclusivity

The model orders institutions by how hard they were to have. This figure places the eight on one scale. The horizontal axis is the share of cities in the comparison pool that ever acquired the institution. The vertical axis is the early-window lead over the average city in the pool.

Figure 6. Early-window lead over the average city against the share of cities acquiring the institution, on log scales, with 95 percent intervals. The paper presents this as an illustration of the model and does not test the relation.

The sharpest contrast is between an institution with a threshold and one without. In Germany guilds and charters were about equally common. Guild cities were ahead and charter towns were not. Exclusivity alone does not separate them. Whether a town had to be large to have the institution does.

Figure 6 uses only the early window and only population, and it measures the lead over the average city rather than over cities without the institution. That is why communes sit near 1 here while Figure 5 shows them ahead. Commune cities were the same size as other cities four and three centuries before the record, and their lead built in the two centuries just before it. Communes were also common. About 58 percent of the cities in the pool acquired one, and when most cities have an institution the average city is mostly made of them, which pulls the lead over the average toward 1.

7What followed the record

The paper's matched event study compares each guild city with never-documented cities in the same guild-strength region whose population one century before documentation was within 1.0 log point of the guild cities' mean. There are 26 experiments, 617 guild cities, and 918 control cities.

Without matching, guild cities rise before their first record and decline after it. That decline is what made guilds look like a brake on growth. With matching, pre-trends are flat in the three centuries before documentation, with a small negative deviation in the fourth. The premium is 14.4 percent at documentation and holds for four centuries, because the matched controls revert toward the mean at the same rate. Where documentation is sustained the premium reaches about 0.31 log points. Where it lapses the premium disappears.

The premium is an association. The century is the resolution of the data, and a city can take off and acquire documented guilds within the same hundred years.

8Cross-city and within-city

Figure 9. Long-run association between a century of guild presence and city size implied by each design, in percent. The cross-city figure extrapolates a growth differential through the estimated convergence rate. The within-city figure is from a two-way fixed effects regression with a lagged dependent variable. Appendix G of the paper reports further specifications.

Cross-city growth regressions find guild cities growing about 7 points faster per century. Pushed through a convergence rate of about a tenth per century, that implies a steady-state city twice as large, which no urban institution could plausibly deliver. Within-city regressions and the matched design both give about 14 percent. The gap between them is the level selection of Section 4. Run backward, the same regressions show it directly. A doubling of a city's size predicts about four more years of documented guild activity in the following century.

9Limits

Market rights and charters are observed only for German and Austrian towns, because the Deutsches Städtebuch is the one source that dates them for many towns. The sharpest comparison in the paper is therefore also the narrowest.

Size and prominence do not always agree. Commune cities were ordinary in size and unusually prominent. Cities that received new sees were large and no more prominent than cities their size. The paper reports both measures.

Notable births are sparse before 1400, so the early comparisons for the rarer institutions are imprecise.

The model orders institutions with two dials and one persistence rate. It predicts selection on levels and says nothing about what an institution does once it exists.
