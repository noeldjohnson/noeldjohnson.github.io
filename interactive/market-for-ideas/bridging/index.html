Bridging

Part of the interactive companion to Mapping the Market for Ideas in Europe, 1450–1650, by Noel Johnson and Alexander Taylor

Bridging asks whether a title sat inside one established subject or between two. It is the second of the two measures we use to describe the titles a larger market added. The first is novelty.

Why it matters

In Weitzman's (1998) account new ideas are combinations of existing ones. Jacobs (1969) put the crossing of ideas between fields behind the growth of cities, and Glaeser et al. (1992) found that industrial diversity predicted the growth of American cities. A title that joins two subjects is a combination in the plainest sense. Bridging counts how much of a city's print was of that kind.

Step 1. Find the subjects

We do not use the catalog's subject classes, which are too broad. We let the titles sort themselves. For each language the \(k\)-means algorithm groups the vectors of every title into 30 clusters. It starts from 30 trial centers, assigns each title to its nearest center, moves each center to the average of its titles, and repeats until the assignments settle. The clusters are fitted once per language on all titles and labeled by the vocabulary words nearest each center.

The clusters are recognizable subjects. In Latin they include grammars, confessional manuals, lectures on the Code and the Decretals, polyphony, funeral orations, medical disputations, and Lutheran polemic. In German they include news, catechisms, plague remedies, funeral sermons, and almanacs. The companion maps the 30 German clusters in its Figure 14.

Step 2. Compare the nearest subject with the second nearest

For each title compute its cosine with every cluster center. Call the highest \(s_1\) and the second highest \(s_2\).

$$\mathrm{Bridging}_i \;=\; \frac{s_2}{s_1}$$

A title deep inside one subject is much closer to its own center than to any other, so the ratio is low. A title halfway between two subjects is equally close to both and the ratio is near one. A city's value for a period is the mean over its doctrine titles.

What the paper finds

Within cities a rise of one within-city standard deviation in vernacular market access went with doctrine titles that sat between subjects by 0.45 standard deviations. The slope is 0.310 with a standard error of 0.054. German shows the largest response. Latin bridging does not move with market access.

The number of clusters is a choice and the result does not depend on it. We use 30 because at 20 one German cluster collects the place names of Lüneburg and Brunswick and one Latin cluster collects the wording of edition statements.

What to keep in mind

  • The ratio is high for a title equally close to two subjects, whether it is close to both or far from both. The peak at 280 degrees in the figure is a title far from every subject. A high value says a title has no single home.
  • The subjects are the catalog's own. They come from how titles group in each language and from no outside classification.
  • The subjects are fixed for the whole period. A title of 1520 and a title of 1640 are placed against the same 30 centers.
  • Most German print was religious, so the second subject of a scientific title is often a religious one. The companion's examples show this.
  • Bridging and novelty rise together with diversity in the data. The three come from the same titles, so that association is descriptive.
