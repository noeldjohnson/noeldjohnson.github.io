Novelty

Part of the interactive companion to Mapping the Market for Ideas in Europe, 1450–1650, by Noel Johnson and Alexander Taylor

Diversity says a better-connected city printed a wider range of titles. It does not say whether the added titles were new or were reprints of what other cities already had. Novelty answers that. It gives every title a number that says how far it sits from anything printed before it in the same language.

Why it matters

In models of growth through new varieties the fixed cost of a variety is the cost of an idea and a larger market is what covers it (Romer 1990). A larger market for print should then bring titles that did not exist before. Text-based measures of this kind are used for patents, where a patent's novelty is its distance from the patents that came before it (Kelly et al. 2021).

The definition

Take a title \(i\) printed in decade \(t\). Compare it with every title printed in the same language in an earlier decade and find the one with the highest cosine similarity. Novelty is one minus that cosine.

$$\mathrm{Novelty}_i \;=\; 1 - \max_{j \,:\, t_j < t_i} \cos(v_i, v_j)$$

A reprint has an identical or nearly identical title to something earlier and scores near zero. A title unlike anything printed before scores high. A city's value for a period is the mean over its doctrine titles, so it does not drift with the number of titles the city printed. The vectors are explained here.

A larger stock leaves less room

Novelty is measured against the stock of earlier titles, so the size of that stock matters. The next figure scatters earlier titles at random around the circle and asks how novel a new title is on average.

Figure 2. A toy calculation in two dimensions. Left, one random stock of earlier titles. Right, the average novelty of a new title drawn at random, by the size of the stock, averaged over 300 stocks. The numbers are illustrative.

This is how we read the Latin result. The Latin reference corpus holds 273,168 titles against 87,800 in German and 57,951 in Italian. A title that a Latin city had not yet printed was far more often a title another city already had.

What the paper finds

Within cities a rise of one within-city standard deviation in vernacular market access went with doctrine titles further from anything printed before in their language by 0.37 standard deviations. The slope is 0.240 with a standard error of 0.081. German shows the largest response at 0.82 standard deviations. Latin novelty does not move with market access although Latin diversity rises. A better-connected Latin press added range from the continent's existing stock. A better-connected vernacular press added titles new to its language.

What to keep in mind

  • Novelty is novelty of a title. A new title can announce an old argument and an old title can carry a new one.
  • The reference is the catalog. A title is new relative to the editions that survive and were recorded.
  • Two kinds of title are left out. Those whose vector coincides with that of an earlier title with different words, and those built from fewer than two words in the vocabulary.
  • The measure describes what a larger market added. Whether those titles changed what a city later produced is a question the paper does not reach.

The companion reports the slopes in its section on what the added variety was.
