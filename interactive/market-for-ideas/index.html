Some of the constructions here will be new to economists. Five short explainers cover them, each with a worked example you can move. Word and title embeddings · Market access · Diversity and distinctiveness · Novelty · Bridging

1Argument

Between 1450 and 1650 European print grew from a Mainz craft producing about 3,000 Latin editions a decade into a market of nearly 800,000 editions in 1,338 cities. As the number of printing cities and the populations around them grew each press came within reach of more readers. We ask whether that integration made what a city printed broader and more like the rest of its market or narrower and more its own.

Two accounts of a growing market give opposite answers. For Adam Smith the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market (Smith 1776, Stigler 1951). A printing city with better access to readers should concentrate on a narrower range of titles and set itself further apart from other cities. Monopolistic competition says a larger market supports more producers of more varieties (Krugman 1980). Models of spatial product differentiation add that a producer with good access to its market positions near that market's center (Hotelling 1929, Anderson, de Palma, and Thisse 1992). A better-connected city should print a wider range of titles and move toward what the rest of its market printed.

We measure both from the titles of the Universal Short Title Catalogue (USTC 2022) and relate them to a panel of market access (Donaldson and Hornbeck 2016). In doctrine, the religious, scholarly, and literary print a press could sell beyond its own city, a better-connected city printed a wider range of titles and moved nearer the mainstream of its language. The response is strongest in German and Italian. The titles a larger vernacular market added were further from anything the language had printed before and lay between established subjects.

The response appeared where printers were free to enter. German and Italian were printed across hundreds of polities where no single authority licensed the trade and they carry both results. French and Spanish came under royal licensing in the 1550s and 1560s and show neither.

2Titles as vectors

The catalog gives each edition a short title, a place and year of printing, a language, and one of about 20 subject classes. The largest class, Religious, holds 215,000 titles and puts Lutheran polemic from Wittenberg beside Catholic devotional manuals from Lyon and Calvinist catechisms from Geneva. We measure content with word embeddings trained on the titles themselves (Mikolov et al. 2013, Pennington, Socher, and Manning 2014). A word is represented by the words it appears near so words used in similar titles end up close together. Ours come from a matrix of word associations reduced to 200 dimensions (Levy and Goldberg 2014). Each language's vectors are trained separately on its own titles.

3Two measures of what a city printed

For each city, language, and decade with at least five titles we compute two numbers and average them over 50-year periods. Diversity is the average cosine distance between pairs of the city's titles. A city printing across many subjects has titles far apart from one another and a city printing a tight niche has titles close together. Distinctiveness is the distance between the city's average title and the average title of every other city in the same language and decade, corrected for the noise in an average taken over few titles.

The figure shows both for Latin print in 1600 to 1609. Paris printed 1,041 Latin titles across theology, classical authors, law, and history and has a diversity of 0.97. Coimbra printed 74, mostly scholastic philosophy and theology, and has 0.71, the lowest of any city shown. Venice printed religion, music, law, and medicine and is the most distinctive at 1.38. Wittenberg was the largest Latin center of the decade with 2,017 titles and sits at 0.76, below the median.

Distinctiveness says how far a city's print sits from the rest of its language. The city's signature says in which direction. It is the set of words whose vectors point most nearly from the rest of the language toward the city's average title.

4Framework

The hypothesis is that the early modern book trade worked like a market for differentiated goods. The framework joins monopolistic competition (Dixit and Stiglitz 1977, Krugman 1979, 1980, Helpman and Krugman 1985) to spatial product differentiation (Hotelling 1929, Lancaster 1979, Salop 1979, Anderson, de Palma, and Thisse 1992). A title is a variety and its position is its place in the embedding. A printer sells to readers in every city, and demand from each city arrives discounted by the cost of shipping. Six assumptions carry the model.

  1. Readers value variety.
  2. Each title has a fixed cost of typesetting and editing that is large relative to the cost of a copy.
  3. Readers in different cities want different titles.
  4. Shipping costs rise with distance and books still move between cities.
  5. Printers are free to enter so a title is printed wherever its sales cover its fixed cost.
  6. Printers choose what to print in light of the demand they face.

The first prediction is that diversity rises with market access. A title for tastes far from the center of a market covers its fixed cost only when enough readers are within reach so a larger market adds titles further from the center. This needs assumptions 1, 2, 4, and 5. The second prediction is that distinctiveness falls with market access where the market is bounded by language. A vernacular market has a center, the cities where most of its readers live, and a printer whose access to those cities rises moves toward them. Latin was sold to the whole continent through the fairs so a Latin printer's market had no nearer center and the prediction is no fall.

Both predictions concern a product a printer can ship. Doctrine kept its value for years and traveled through the fairs. News lost its value within weeks and sold where it was printed so the framework predicts that neither measure of news moves with market access. News is the comparison.

The framework also says where the predictions should fail. A central authority that licensed entry and fixed where presses worked breaks assumptions 5 and 6 and with them both predictions. Section 8 takes this up.

5Market access

A city's market access sums the populations of other cities, each discounted by the cost of reaching it (Donaldson and Hornbeck 2016, Donaldson 2018). Populations are from Buringh (2021) and Bosker, Buringh, and van Zanden (2013). Costs come from least-cost paths over roads, rivers, and seas, with sea travel cheapest and overland portage dearest, on the cost schedule of Bairoch (1988). A city's own population is left out so its own growth does not count as access to readers. For a vernacular the sum runs over the cities that printed in that language and for Latin over the university cities.

Market access changes within a city because populations elsewhere change. Paris grew from about 94,000 people in 1500 to about 387,000 in 1650 and Amsterdam from about 12,000 to about 104,000, while Bruges fell from about 35,000 to about 27,000 by 1600.

6Diversity and distinctiveness

We ask how a city's print in a language changed when its market access rose relative to that city's own average and to the change common to all cities in the period. Each slope compares a city with itself over time so a city's university, its size, and its position on the trade routes do not enter. The panel has 1,876 observations of a city, a language, and a 50-year period, across 504 cities. Doctrine has 1,578 across 441.

$$y_{j\ell d} \;=\; \beta \,\log \mathrm{MA}_{j\ell d} \;+\; \alpha_{j\ell} \;+\; \delta_{d} \;+\; \varepsilon_{j\ell d}$$

Here \(y_{j\ell d}\) is the diversity or the distinctiveness of city \(j\)'s titles in language \(\ell\) and period \(d\), and \(\log \mathrm{MA}_{j\ell d}\) is the log of its market access in that language. The city-and-language effect \(\alpha_{j\ell}\) removes the city's own average and the period effect \(\delta_d\) removes the change common to all cities. Each observation is weighted by the square root of its number of titles and standard errors are clustered by city.

Explainer. The estimating equation, its weights and standard errors, and its extensions.

Choose a product and a measure. Magnitudes in standard deviations are for a rise of one within-city standard deviation of log market access, 0.16 log units, about the rise a typical city saw over half a century.

Figure 6. Within-city slope of each measure on log market access, pooled and by language. Weighted two-way fixed effects with city-and-language and period effects. Intervals are 95 percent and use the larger of the city-clustered standard error and a standard error that allows correlation between cities within 250 km (Conley 1999). Filled points have intervals that exclude zero. Latin doctrine excludes academic dissertations.

Doctrine diversity rises. Pooled over six languages the move goes with diversity higher by 0.39 standard deviations. The response is 1.28 standard deviations in German, 0.63 in Italian, and 0.30 in Latin. Over all titles, news included, the pooled rise is a quarter of a standard deviation.

Vernacular doctrine distinctiveness falls. Pooled over the five vernaculars the same move brings a city's doctrine nearer the mainstream of its language by 0.32 standard deviations, and by 1.35 in German and 0.86 in Italian. Latin doctrine shows no fall. Its interval excludes the German and Italian falls and contains zero.

News shows neither pattern. The move that raises vernacular doctrine diversity by 0.34 standard deviations raises news diversity by 0.12 and news distinctiveness rises by 0.15, both imprecisely. The framework does not sign practical print, the medical, agricultural, military, and technical handbooks.

7How far books traveled

Market access depends on how fast it discounts distant cities. The trade elasticity sets that rate. A low value counts readers far away nearly as much as readers nearby and a high value counts mainly the neighbors. The framework puts the diversity response at a low elasticity, where distant readers count, and the pull toward the center at an intermediate one. Our headline value is 1.

Figure 8. Doctrine slopes per within-city standard deviation of log market access, by trade elasticity, with 95 percent intervals clustered by city. Pooled is six languages for diversity and the five vernaculars for distinctiveness.

Pooled doctrine diversity is larger at elasticities of 0.5 and 0.75 than at 1 and falls toward zero from 3. Distinctiveness is most negative between 0.75 and 1. Books were a good with low trade costs and the data place the response where such a good should have it.

8Where printers could not enter

German and Italian were printed across hundreds of polities. Venice and the Empire had censors but no single authority licensed the trade across either language and a printer refused in one state could print in another. They are 52 percent of vernacular titles. French and Spanish are a further 36 percent. The ordinances of Mantes in 1563 and Moulins in 1566 made royal permission the condition of printing a book in France (Isambert, Decrusy, and Taillandier 1829). The pragmática of 1558 required every book printed in Castile to be licensed by the Council of Castile (Reyes Gómez 2000). A license that can be refused caps entry.

Figure 9. Within-city slopes on log market access with German and Italian pooled and with French and Spanish pooled. Intervals as in Figure 6.

Where entry was free a rise of one within-city standard deviation in market access went with doctrine diversity higher by 0.92 standard deviations. The slope is 0.683 with a standard error of 0.111. Where the crown licensed the presses the same rise went with a change of 0.03 standard deviations, a slope of 0.022 with a standard error of 0.087. The licensing slope is close to zero and precisely estimated. Estimated together the difference between the two is −0.66 with a standard error of 0.14. Distinctiveness fell by 0.80 standard deviations where entry was free and did not move under licensing.

Six languages are six observations so the regimes are a reading of the framework and not a test of it. Castile offers one comparison within a language. In decade cells the Spanish doctrine diversity slope is positive before 1560 and negative from 1560.

Figure 10. The Spanish diversity slope on log market access in decade cells, before and from the cut, with 95 percent intervals clustered by city. This is one case. The Valdés Index of 1559 coincides with the pragmática and the timing alone cannot separate them.

A border can. The Council of Castile licensed books printed in Castile. The Inquisition and its Index covered the Crown of Aragon as well. Castilian output fell from 436 records in 1550 to 1554 to 189 in 1555 to 1559. Aragon's share of the Spanish-language printing in Spain was 14 to 18 percent in every five-year bin from 1540 to 1554 and 27 to 37 percent in every bin from 1560 to 1599. The Castilian fall began in 1555, before the law.

Figure 11. Spanish-language catalog records with a recorded place and year, in five-year bins from 1540 to 1599. The dashed line marks the pragmática of 1558 and the Index of 1559.

9What the added variety was

A larger market added titles. In models of growth through new varieties a larger market is what covers the fixed cost of a new idea (Romer 1990), and new ideas are also combinations of existing ones (Weitzman 1998). Two measures ask whether the added titles were of those kinds. The novelty of a title is one minus its cosine with the nearest title printed earlier in the same language. A reprint scores near zero. Bridging uses 30 subject clusters per language. It is the title's cosine with its second-nearest cluster divided by its cosine with the nearest. A title inside one subject scores low and a title between two subjects scores near one.

These are the inputs that accounts of idea production take as given. A new variety is the good whose fixed cost a larger market covers and a title between subjects is a combination of existing ones. The crossing of ideas between fields is what Jacobs (1969) and Glaeser et al. (1992) put behind the growth of cities. Whether a city with such titles grew or produced more ideas is a question the paper does not reach.

10What the results say about policy

A license to print is a rule about who may enter a market. The results of Section 8 give its cost in the unit that matters to readers, the variety a larger market would otherwise have produced. The literature on occupational licensing measures the cost of entry rules in wages and employment (Kleiner and Krueger 2013). In a market for ideas the cost is the range of titles that were never printed, and it can be read from the text of what was printed.

The calculator applies the two slopes of Section 8 to a rise in market access you choose. Readers bore that cost. In German and Italian a reader's gain from a larger market came as a wider range of titles printed nearby. In France and Castile the same growth in reach brought no such range. Readers there lost the variety a larger market would have brought, less what they could buy from presses outside the license's reach. The Crown of Aragon was one such place. Its share of the Spanish-language printing in Spain rose from under a fifth to about a third after 1558.

Political fragmentation kept most of the European market free to enter (Jones 1981, Mokyr 2017). The state that could license every press was the state that could enforce conformity (Johnson and Koyama 2019), and the costs of that enforcement are measurable in Spanish municipalities centuries later (Drelichman, Vidal-Robert, and Voth 2021).

Three modern markets have the same structure.

  • Platforms. One fear about cheaper distribution is that it fragments a public into narrower audiences (Sunstein 2017). In the early modern market, where reach grew and entry was free, producers broadened their range and moved toward the center of demand at once. Online the ideological segregation of news readers is modest (Gentzkow and Shapiro 2011). A platform that decides who may produce holds the position the Council of Castile held and the prediction for that market is the Castilian one (Stigler Committee on Digital Platforms 2019). Cheaper reach changes little where the response to a larger market runs through entry.
  • Local news. News is the product that did not respond to reach. It sold where it was printed and a larger market neither broadened it nor pulled it toward a center. The modern counterpart is local journalism, a product sold where it is made, whose decline tracks the entry and exit of newspapers more than the reach of the ones that remain (Gentzkow, Shapiro, and Sinkinson 2011, Hayes and Lawless 2021).
  • Scientific publishing. Latin shows what a market that is already continental does when its producers' reach improves. Better access did not move Latin print toward any center while its diversity rose. Scientific publishing is one worldwide market in one language whose distribution cost has collapsed and in which five firms publish more than half of all papers (Larivière, Haustein, and Mongeon 2015). A scholarly market with a gatekeeper has the Latin geography and the Castilian precondition.

These are the paper's readings of its results. The regime comparison rests on six languages and the Castilian timing is a single case, so the policy readings carry the same caveat. The paper reads its slopes as associations.
