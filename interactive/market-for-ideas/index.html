Some of the constructions here will be new to economists. Six short explainers cover them, each with a worked example you can move. Word and title embeddings · Market access · Diversity and distinctiveness · Novelty · Bridging

1Argument

Between 1450 and 1650 European print grew from a Mainz craft producing about 3,000 Latin editions a decade into a market of nearly 800,000 editions in 1,338 cities. As the number of printing cities and the populations around them grew each press came within reach of more readers. We ask whether that integration made what a city printed broader and more like the rest of its market or narrower and more its own.

Two accounts of a growing market give opposite answers. For Adam Smith the division of labor is limited by the extent of the market (Smith 1776, Stigler 1951). A printing city with better access to readers should concentrate on a narrower range of titles and set itself farther apart from other cities. Monopolistic competition says a larger market supports more producers of more varieties (Krugman 1980). Models of spatial product differentiation add that a producer with good access to its market positions near that market's center (Hotelling 1929, Anderson, de Palma, and Thisse 1992). A better-connected city should print a wider range of titles and move toward what the rest of its market printed.

We measure both from the titles of the Universal Short Title Catalogue (USTC 2022) and relate them to a panel of market access (Donaldson and Hornbeck 2016). In doctrine, the religious, scholarly, and literary print a press could sell beyond its own city, a better-connected city printed a wider range of titles and moved nearer the mainstream of its language. The titles it added were farther from anything the language had printed before and lay between established subjects. Most were sermons, catechisms, and controversy. A small share were instrument books, natural histories, and mechanical treatises, what Mokyr (2017) calls useful knowledge.

These results appear where no single authority licensed a language's presses. German, Italian, and Dutch were printed under many licensing authorities and are nearly two-thirds of vernacular print. French and Spanish were each licensed by one crown. There a larger market brought no wider range and no rise in new titles.

2Titles as vectors

The catalog gives each edition a short title, a place and year of printing, a language, and one of about 20 subject classes. The largest class, Religious, holds 215,000 titles and puts Lutheran polemic from Wittenberg beside Catholic devotional manuals from Lyon and Calvinist catechisms from Geneva. We measure content with word embeddings trained on the titles themselves (Mikolov et al. 2013, Pennington, Socher, and Manning 2014). A word is represented by the words it appears near so words used in similar titles end up close together. Ours come from a matrix of word associations reduced to 200 dimensions (Levy and Goldberg 2014). Each language's vectors are trained separately on its own titles.

3Two measures of what a city printed

For each city, language, and decade with at least five titles we compute two numbers and average them over 50-year periods. Diversity is the average cosine distance between pairs of the city's titles. A city printing across many subjects has titles far apart from one another and a city printing a tight niche has titles close together. Distinctiveness is the distance between the city's average title and the average title of every other city in the same language and decade, corrected for the noise in an average taken over few titles.

The figure shows both for Latin print in 1600 to 1609. Paris printed 1,041 Latin titles across theology, classical authors, law, and history and has a diversity of 0.97. Coimbra printed 74, mostly scholastic philosophy and theology, and has 0.71, the lowest of any city shown. Venice printed religion, music, law, and medicine and is the most distinctive at 1.38. Wittenberg was the largest Latin center of the decade with 2,017 titles and sits at 0.76, below the median.

4Framework

The hypothesis is that the early modern book trade worked like a market for differentiated goods. The framework joins monopolistic competition (Dixit and Stiglitz 1977, Krugman 1979, 1980, Helpman and Krugman 1985) to spatial product differentiation (Hotelling 1929, Lancaster 1979, Salop 1979, Anderson, de Palma, and Thisse 1992). A title is a variety and its position is its place in the embedding. A printer sells to readers in every city and demand from each city arrives discounted by the cost of shipping. Six assumptions carry the model.

  1. Readers value variety.
  2. Each title has a fixed cost of typesetting and editing that is large relative to the cost of a copy.
  3. Readers in different cities want different titles.
  4. Shipping costs rise with distance and books still move between cities. Part of the demand for a title stays with the city that printed it.
  5. Printers are free to enter so a title is printed wherever its sales cover its fixed cost.
  6. Printers choose what to print in light of the demand they face.

The first prediction is that diversity rises with market access. A title for tastes far from the center of a market covers its fixed cost only when enough readers are within reach so a larger market adds titles farther from the center. The second prediction is that distinctiveness falls with market access where the market is bounded by language. A vernacular market has a center, the cities where most of its readers live, and a printer whose access to those cities rises moves toward them. Latin was sold to the whole continent through the fairs so a Latin printer's market had no nearer center and the prediction is no fall.

Both predictions concern a product a printer can ship. Doctrine kept its value for years and traveled through the fairs. News lost its value within weeks and sold where it was printed so the framework predicts that neither measure of news moves with market access. News is the comparison.

The third prediction concerns what the added titles were. Diversity alone does not say. A press can widen its range by reprinting what other cities already sell. In models of growth through new varieties the fixed cost of a variety is the cost of an idea and a larger market is what covers it (Romer 1990). New ideas are also combinations of existing ones (Weitzman 1998). Where market access raises diversity the added titles should be new to the language and lie between established subjects. Where it does not there is no added variety and neither should move. Latin readers shared one body of learning across cities and the Latin market was the largest of the six. The titles a better-connected Latin press adds are then titles other cities already print and neither measure should rise.

Every government in Europe tried to control the press and the controls were of two kinds. Censorship decided what could be read. Licensing decided who could print. Licensing bears on assumptions 4 and 5. The right to print a title reached as far as the authority that granted it. Where a language was printed under many authorities a press could bring out its own edition of a title a neighboring city sold. No one authority could refuse a printer across the whole of the market.

German, Italian, and Dutch were printed under many authorities. In the Empire the enforcement of censorship lay with the territorial rulers and they applied imperial law unevenly (Wilke 2013). The Italian states licensed separately and a printer refused in one could print in another (Grendler 1977). The civil authorities of the Dutch Republic required no license before publication (van Eijnatten 2003). A privilege granted by a Dutch city held only within that city (Buning 2024).

French and Spanish were each printed under one crown. The crown decided who printed a title and its grant ran across the kingdom. A press could not bring out its own edition of a recent title that another press of the kingdom held. In France a royal privilege gave one publisher the sole right to print and sell a new book for five to ten years and the crown favored the leading Paris firms (Martin 1993). In Castile the license of the royal council for a new book carried the sole right to publish and sell it (Kamen 2014). The rights were limited. A French privilege lapsed after its term and in Castile reprints often went ahead without a new license. The restriction fell on recent titles. From 1558 every new book printed in Castile needed a license from the Council of Castile (Reyes Gómez 2000). In France the ordinances of Mantes in 1563 and Moulins in 1566 made royal permission the condition of printing a book (Isambert, Decrusy, and Taillandier 1829).

Under one crown the first two predictions change sign. A recent title is printed by the press that holds it and sold across the kingdom. More readers in a neighboring city then support more titles at the presses that serve them and those titles also sell in the city's own market. A rise in the city's market access adds more to the competition its presses face than to their sales. They are left with what their own readers want and other presses do not print. Their range does not widen and their print moves away from that of other cities. These are the signs of Smith's account, which is the framework's case when one edition serves the whole market. With no added variety the third prediction implies no rise in novelty or bridging. Section 8 takes this up.

5Market access

A city's market access sums the populations of other cities, each discounted by the cost of reaching it (Donaldson and Hornbeck 2016, Donaldson 2018). Populations are from Buringh (2021) and Bosker, Buringh, and van Zanden (2013). Costs come from least-cost paths over roads, rivers, and seas, with sea travel cheapest and overland portage dearest, on the cost schedule of Bairoch (1988). A city's own population is left out so its own growth does not count as access to readers. For a vernacular the sum runs over the cities that printed in that language and for Latin over the university cities.

Market access changes within a city because populations elsewhere change. Paris grew from about 94,000 people in 1500 to about 387,000 in 1650 and Amsterdam from about 12,000 to about 104,000 while Bruges fell from about 35,000 to about 27,000 by 1600.

6Diversity and distinctiveness

We ask how a city's print in a language changed when its market access rose relative to that city's own average and to the change common to the cities of its language in the period. Each slope compares a city with itself over time so a city's university, its size, and its position on the trade routes do not enter. The panel has 1,876 observations of a city, a language, and a 50-year period, across 504 cities. Doctrine has 1,578 across 441.

$$y_{j\ell d} \;=\; \beta \,\log \mathrm{MA}_{j\ell d} \;+\; \alpha_{j\ell} \;+\; \delta_{\ell d} \;+\; \varepsilon_{j\ell d}$$

Here \(y_{j\ell d}\) is the diversity or the distinctiveness of city \(j\)'s titles in language \(\ell\) and period \(d\), and \(\log \mathrm{MA}_{j\ell d}\) is the log of its market access in that language. The city-and-language effect \(\alpha_{j\ell}\) removes the city's own average and the period effect \(\delta_{\ell d}\) removes the change common to the cities of a language. There are three pools. The first holds the six languages. The second holds German, Italian, and Dutch, the vernaculars printed under many licensing authorities. The third holds French and Spanish, each printed under one crown. Each observation is weighted by the square root of its number of titles and standard errors are clustered by city.

Doctrine distinctiveness falls under many authorities. The same move brings a city's doctrine nearer the mainstream of its language by 0.78 standard deviations, and by 1.35 in German and 0.86 in Italian. Latin doctrine shows no fall. Its interval excludes the German and Italian falls and contains zero.

News is the comparison and its range does not widen. Under many authorities the news diversity slope is 0.104, a fifth of the doctrine slope of 0.503, and the doctrine slope lies outside its interval. Latin news diversity does not rise where Latin doctrine diversity does. The framework does not sign practical print, the medical, agricultural, military, and technical handbooks. French and Spanish show neither pattern in doctrine and Section 8 takes them up.

7How far books traveled

Market access depends on how fast it discounts distant cities. The trade elasticity sets that rate. A low value counts readers far away nearly as much as readers nearby and a high value counts mainly the neighbors. The framework puts the diversity response at a low elasticity, where distant readers count, and the pull toward the center at an intermediate one. Our headline value is 1.

Figure 8. Doctrine slopes per within-city standard deviation of log market access, by trade elasticity, with 95 percent intervals clustered by city. Pooled is six languages for diversity and the five vernaculars for distinctiveness.

Doctrine diversity is largest at an elasticity of 0.5 to 0.75 and distinctiveness is most negative between 0.5 and 1. Both are near zero from 3. Books were a good with low trade costs and the data place the response where such a good should have it.

9What the added variety was

Under many authorities a city's doctrine is more diverse where its market access is higher. The third prediction says the added titles are new to the language and lie between established subjects. Two measures of a doctrine title test it. The novelty of a title is one minus its cosine with the nearest title printed earlier in the same language. A reprint scores near zero. Bridging uses 30 subject clusters per language. It is the title's cosine with its second-nearest cluster divided by its cosine with the nearest. A title inside one subject scores low and a title between two subjects scores near one.

Under many authorities both measures rise with market access. A rise of one within-city standard deviation in access goes with doctrine titles that score 0.61 standard deviations higher on bridging and 0.43 higher on novelty. Bridging rises in each of the three languages. Novelty rises in German and Dutch and not in Italian. Under one crown market access does not raise diversity and it does not raise novelty either. The novelty slope is 0.018 with a standard error of 0.202 and the bridging slope 0.196 with a standard error of 0.123.

Figure 14. Doctrine cells with city-and-language and language-by-period means removed, sorted into 20 equal bins of log market access within each group. Points are weighted bin means and the line is the group's slope.

Novelty and bridging came with the diversity. Under many authorities a cell whose doctrine diversity is one within-city standard deviation higher scores 0.40 standard deviations higher on bridging and 0.26 higher on novelty. With diversity held fixed market access adds little to either. The three measures come from the same titles and a shock to the city itself can move all of them. Market access leaves out the city's own population and does not carry such a shock. With access as the instrument for diversity the slopes are 0.799 and 0.453, close to the direct ones. Under one crown diversity varies for reasons other than market access and it comes with less novelty and bridging.

Latin diversity rises with market access and neither measure does. The Latin stock was large, 273,168 titles against 87,800 in German and 57,951 in Italian, so a title a Latin city had not yet printed was far more often a title another city had. A larger Latin market brought a city more of what the continent already printed. A larger German market brought titles new to the language.

The most novel titles were of every kind. Of the 4,802 German doctrine titles in the top tenth of novelty 3,824 are religious works and 98 fall in the catalog's classes for science, mathematics, astrology, and botany. Most of the titles between subjects are religious. Of the 4,802 German doctrine titles in the top tenth of bridging 3,705 are religious works and 97 fall in the same four classes. A Catholic catechism printed at Mainz in 1555 lies between catechisms and confessional controversy. A wedding sermon on Hebrews 13 printed at Nuremberg in 1531 lies between funeral sermons on a Bible verse and sermons on Gospel chapters. A larger market brought more new titles and more combinations of every kind and a small share of them carried what Mokyr (2017) calls useful knowledge.

New varieties and combinations are the inputs that accounts of idea production take as given. A new variety is the good whose fixed cost a larger market covers and a title between subjects is a combination of existing ones. The crossing of ideas between fields is what Jacobs (1969) and Glaeser et al. (1992) put behind the growth of cities. Most of the combinations were sermons, catechisms, and controversy. A few were instrument books and natural histories. Whether a city with such titles grew or produced more ideas is a question the paper does not reach.

10How large the response was

The slopes above are in standard deviations. This section restates them as titles. The calculations are ours, made from the paper's estimates for this page, and they are not in the paper.

Call a doctrine title novel if it is in the top quarter of novelty in its language and bridging if it is in the top quarter of bridging. In the average city 41 percent of doctrine titles are one or the other. Under many authorities a rise of one within-city standard deviation in market access takes that share from 41 to 50 percent. Under one crown it stays at 41 percent. In a city that printed 255 doctrine titles in half a century the rise under many authorities is about 21 more titles that are novel or bridging. Under one crown it is none.

11What the results say about policy

A license to print is a rule about who may enter a market. The results of Section 8 give its cost in the unit that matters to readers, the variety a larger market would otherwise have produced. The literature on occupational licensing measures the cost of entry rules in wages and employment (Kleiner and Krueger 2013). In a market for ideas the cost is the range of titles that were never printed and it can be read from the text of what was printed.

Readers bore that cost. In German, Italian, and Dutch a reader's gain from a larger market came as a wider range of titles printed nearby. In France and Castile the same growth in reach brought no such range. Readers there lost the variety a larger market would have brought, less what they could buy from presses outside the license's reach. The Crown of Aragon and Navarre were such places. Their share of the Spanish-language printing in Spain went from under a fifth to about a third as Castilian output fell.

Political fragmentation kept most of the European market open to entry (Jones 1981, Mokyr 2017). The state that could license every press was the state that could enforce conformity (Johnson and Koyama 2019). The costs of that enforcement are measurable in Spanish municipalities centuries later (Drelichman, Vidal-Robert, and Voth 2021).

Three modern markets have the same structure.

  • Platforms. One fear about cheaper distribution is that it fragments a public into narrower audiences (Sunstein 2017). In the early modern market, where reach grew and no single authority controlled entry, producers broadened their range and moved toward the center of demand at once. Online the ideological segregation of news readers is modest (Gentzkow and Shapiro 2011). A platform that decides who may produce holds the position the Council of Castile held and the prediction for that market is the Castilian one (Stigler Committee on Digital Platforms 2019). Cheaper reach changes little where the response to a larger market runs through entry.
  • Local news. News is the product that did not respond to reach. It sold where it was printed and a larger market neither broadened it nor pulled it toward a center. The modern counterpart is local journalism, a product sold where it is made, whose decline tracks the entry and exit of newspapers more than the reach of the ones that remain (Gentzkow, Shapiro, and Sinkinson 2011, Hayes and Lawless 2021).
  • Scientific publishing. Latin shows what a market that is already continental does when its producers' reach improves. Better access did not move Latin print toward any center while its diversity rose. Scientific publishing is one worldwide market in one language whose distribution cost has collapsed and in which five firms publish more than half of all papers (Larivière, Haustein, and Mongeon 2015). A scholarly market with a gatekeeper has the geography of the Latin market and the controlled entry of Castile.
